\begin{abstract}
Commercial service robots are increasingly sold as closed Android--\ros{} systems: no public
SDK, no message schemas, and a vendor cloud the owner cannot replace, so a laboratory that owns
one cannot even establish which commands it accepts. We present a non-destructive methodology
for recovering that command surface without vendor cooperation: four falsifiable hypotheses
about protocol observability and verification, resolved through a seven-phase pipeline of
network mapping, \rosapi{} introspection, static analysis of the vendor application, and passive
broker observation. Validated on a commercial reception robot, it recovers enough of the command
surface to rebuild teleoperation, autonomous navigation, speech output and visitor interaction as
an edge platform operating without the vendor cloud. The main finding concerns verification, not
discovery: a transport-level acknowledgment reveals nothing about physical execution. Across
\num{17} logged field commands, the reported localization state alone dictated the outcome---all
ten failures unlocalized, all seven successes navigating---while addressing scheme showed no
effect, confirmed by a bench comparison in which two schemes expected to differ succeeded equally
often ($p=1.00$) in equal time ($p=0.76$) once preconditions held. Reaching this required
correcting our own instrumentation, which had invoked an unexposed firmware service. We report
field indicators with confidence intervals, a capability comparison against the vendor
application, and transferability conditions for other closed platforms.
\end{abstract}
