\begin{abstract}
Commercial service robots are increasingly sold as closed Android--\ros{} systems: no public
SDK, no message schemas, and a vendor cloud that the owner cannot replace. A laboratory that
owns such a robot cannot modify its interaction behaviour, nor establish which commands the
machine accepts. We present a non-destructive methodology for recovering that command surface
without vendor cooperation. Structured as four falsifiable hypotheses regarding protocol
observability and verification, our approach is evaluated through a seven-phase pipeline that
triangulates network mapping, \rosapi{} introspection, static analysis of the vendor
application, and passive broker observation. We validate the methodology on a commercial
reception robot, recovering enough of its command surface to rebuild teleoperation, autonomous
navigation, speech output, and visitor interaction as an edge platform operating without the
vendor cloud. Our primary empirical finding concerns verification rather than discovery: a
transport-level acknowledgment reveals nothing about physical execution. In \num{17} logged
field commands, the execution outcome was dictated entirely by the reported localization state.
All ten failures occurred while unlocalized, and all seven successes occurred while navigating.
Addressing scheme choice showed no effect, and a controlled bench comparison confirms that two
addressing schemes expected to differ succeed equally often ($p=1.00$) in equal time ($p=0.76$)
once preconditions hold. Reaching this conclusion required correcting our own instrumentation,
which initially invoked an unexposed firmware service. We report field indicators with
confidence intervals, a capability comparison against the vendor application, and transferability
conditions for other closed platforms.
\end{abstract}
